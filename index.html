How it works

Everything is figured in today's dollars: returns are discounted by inflation, so a dollar here always buys what a dollar buys now.

Your freedom date is the first year you could stop and still have money left at the end of the plan — not a rule of thumb, an actual year-by-year run of the money. The balance keeps compounding after you stop; only what you spend leaves.

Health cover before Medicare is charged on top of your spending in every retired year up to 65, in today's dollars. ACA subsidies can cut that bill sharply if your reported income is low, and this does not model them — set it to what you expect to actually pay.

The history panel re-runs that same plan on real returns: S&P 500 with dividends, 10-year Treasuries and CPI for every year from 1928 to 2024, blended to your stock/bond mix and rebalanced once a year. Each run starts in a different year and takes the returns in the order they came. Source: Damodaran, NYU Stern.

Split the accounts and withdrawals go brokerage first, then the 401(k) grossed up for tax, then Roth. A 10% penalty applies to 401(k) money taken before 59½, and required minimum distributions start at 73 or 75 depending on your birth year.

What it ignores

Fees, a paid-off house, a bad decade at exactly the wrong moment, and the raise you're about to get. Leave the accounts un-split and it ignores tax entirely.

Capital-gains tax on the brokerage account, state tax, and the Roth ladders and 72(t) withdrawals that can sidestep the early-withdrawal penalty.

The historical runs overlap heavily — a 58-year plan only fits 40 times into 97 years of data, and neighbouring runs share most of their years. Treat the count as a stress test, not a probability.

This is a model, not advice. Real markets don't return the same number every year, and neither will yours.
